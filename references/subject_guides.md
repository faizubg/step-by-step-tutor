## 1. Mathematics Example: Adding Fractions with Unlike Denominators

### Lesson Block
```markdown
### Adding Fractions: Step 1 - Finding Common Ground

1. **Big Idea**: You cannot add fractions directly if their bottom numbers (denominators) are different, because their pieces are different sizes. You must convert them into equal-sized pieces first.
2. **Real-Life Example**: Imagine adding 1 slice of a 4-slice pizza to 1 slice of an 8-slice pizza. They aren't equal sizes! To add them fairly, you cut the bigger slice into 2 smaller pieces so every piece is the same eighth-size.
3. **Worked Example**:
   - Problem: Calculate 1/3 + 1/6
   - Step 1: Find a common denominator. Multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9... and 6 is already an option. Use 6.
   - Step 2: Convert 1/3 to sixths by multiplying top and bottom by 2: (1 × 2) / (3 × 2) = 2/6.
   - Step 3: Add the top numbers (numerators): 2/6 + 1/6 = (2 + 1)/6 = 3/6.
   - Result: Simplify 3/6 by dividing top and bottom by 3: 1/2.
4. **Your Turn**: What is 1/4 + 1/2? Try converting 1/2 into fourths first!
```

### Feedback & Recap Block (After Learner Answers: "3/4")
```markdown
### Feedback
- Excellent work! You converted 1/2 to 2/4 and correctly added 1/4 + 2/4 to get 3/4.

### Practice Set
1. 1/5 + 2/10 = ?
2. 2/3 + 1/6 = ?
3. 1/2 + 1/3 = ? (Hint: Find a number both 2 and 3 divide into evenly)

### 3-Line Recap
- **Core Concept**: Fractions must share equal slice sizes (common denominators) before adding.
- **Key Strategy**: Multiply numerator and denominator by the same number to create matching bottoms.
- **Pitfall to Avoid**: Never add the bottom numbers together (e.g., 1/3 + 1/6 is not 2/9).
```

## 2. English Grammar Example: Subject-Verb Agreement with Collective Nouns

### Lesson Block
```markdown
### English Grammar: Subject-Verb Agreement with Groups

1. **Big Idea**: A collective noun names a single group made of multiple members (like *team*, *family*, or *class*). In standard English, when the group acts together as one single unit, it takes a singular verb.
2. **Real-Life Example**: Think of a choir singing on stage. Even though there are 20 people singing, you hear one blended song from one choir.
3. **Worked Example**:
   - Problem: Choose the correct verb: "The jury (has / have) reached its verdict."
   - Step 1: Identify the subject: *The jury*. It is acting together as one single body.
   - Step 2: Notice the pronoun clue *its* (singular), which confirms unity.
   - Step 3: Match with the singular verb: *has*.
   - Result: "The jury has reached its verdict."
4. **Your Turn**: Fill in the blank with *is* or *are*: "The committee ___ meeting in the conference room today."
```

### Feedback & Recap Block (After Learner Answers: "is")
```markdown
### Feedback
- Spot on! The committee is acting together as one body, so the singular verb "is" is correct.

### Practice Set
1. "The orchestra (tunes / tune) their individual instruments before the show." (Notice: individual actions!)
2. "My family (enjoys / enjoy) Sunday dinners together."
3. "The herd of cattle (was / were) grazing on the open hillside."

### 3-Line Recap
- **Core Concept**: Collective nouns take singular verbs when the group acts as a unified whole.
- **Key Strategy**: Look at the action and pronouns—if the group moves as one, treat it as "it" (singular).
- **Pitfall to Avoid**: Do not mistake having multiple members inside a group for a plural subject.
```

## 3. University Example: Economics (Opportunity Cost)

### Calibrating for University Students
- Maintain academic precision while avoiding unnecessary jargon.
- Retain the same 4-part structure to preserve focus and clarity.

### Lesson Block
```markdown
### Microeconomics: Opportunity Cost

1. **Big Idea**: Opportunity cost is not the total sum of all alternatives; it is the value of the next best alternative given up when making a decision.
2. **Real-Life Example**: If you have two hours tonight and your top preference is studying for an exam, followed by sleeping as your second choice, the opportunity cost of studying is sleep—not going to the movies, partying, or gaming.
3. **Worked Example**:
   - Scenario: A university invests $1,000,000 in a new research lab instead of keeping it in an endowment earning 5% interest annually ($50,000/year).
   - Step 1: Identify the chosen path: Spending $1,000,000 on the research lab.
   - Step 2: Identify the foregone next-best financial alternative: Earning 5% guaranteed yield in the endowment.
   - Result: The opportunity cost of the lab is the foregone $50,000 annual return on the capital.
4. **Your Turn**: A student gives up a 4-hour shift paying $15/hour to attend a free 4-hour workshop. What is the monetary opportunity cost of attending the workshop?
```
